Same sky. Same color.
Then ask what changed.

A young man blinked two photographic plates and found Pluto. You cannot blink SPHEREx. Each pixel of each exposure sees a different color of infrared light, so two pictures of one star can differ even when nothing in the sky changed.

CHRONOS compares only fair pairs, then asks five questions of every change. What changed? Where? Is it real? What is it? What would prove us wrong? Three independent methods look for change. Where they disagree, CHRONOS investigates the disagreement.

For watchers of the changing sky like Radhagobinda Chandra of Jessore, who recorded variable stars by eye for decades, and for anyone who wants to check a claim instead of trusting it.

II

Fair pairs: the SPHEREx rule

A linear variable filter gives each pixel its own color. Brightness can only be compared between exposures of the same array at nearly the same color. Each dot is one exposure. A line joins two visits that form a fair pair.

Calibration guardian

Release of every visit on every array, and the gain that the catalog stars measure. A release change or a gain step must not become a discovery.

III

The difference microscope

One pair of exposures, then the same pair with one more correction at each layer. Watch the false changes fall away. Only what survives honest noise in a fair pair counts.

The motion cartographer

Every measured track on the deep image, drawn larger than life. Ellipses show one sigma position errors. μ = √((Δα cos δ)² + Δδ²) / Δt.

VII

Validation: how often is CHRONOS wrong?

Hundreds of practice fields with a hidden truth. The engine never saw the test fields. Intervals are 95 percent Wilson intervals.

VIII

IX

X

XII

The whole sky, in tiles

A change map corrected for coverage. The sky is cut into equal area tiles. Each tile counts the changes the engine calls real, removes the false alarms of a null twin, and divides by the completeness measured with planted sources. Practice sky.
