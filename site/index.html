A physics project on NASA's SPHEREx infrared survey

Pluto was found by blinking two photographs. SPHEREx cannot be blinked.

Every pixel of SPHEREx sees its own colour of infrared light. A star that never changed still looks brighter or fainter when it lands on a different pixel. CHRONOS is the physics and the software that decide whether a change is in the sky, or only in the colour, the camera or the calibration.

The physics: a rainbow glued to the detector

A linear variable filter sits on each of the six SPHEREx arrays. Its colour changes smoothly from one edge to the other, so the wavelength a pixel sees depends on where the star lands. Because the resolving power R = λ/Δλ is constant along a band, the wavelength grows geometrically across the array:

λ(x) = λmin (λmax/λmin)x/N,   Δλ = λ/R

Move the two markers. They are where a star landed on visit 1 and visit 2. The spectrum on the right is a blackbody star, optionally behind interstellar ice. The star did not change at all, yet the camera reports a change, because it measured two different colours.

Fair pair rule

same array, |λ₁ − λ₂| ≤ ½ Δλ, one data release

Only such pairs are compared pixel by pixel. Everything else goes through the catalogue method, which measures each visit on its own.

Honest difference

Z = (I₂ − k I₁) / √(σ₂² + k²σ₁² + (f s)²)

k matches the gain using catalogue stars, f is the resampling error measured on stars that did not change, s the local signal. A steady star gives |Z| near 1.

Motion court

BIC = χ²/s² + k ln(2n)

Three models of every position track: fixed (k = 2), linear motion (k = 4), motion plus annual parallax (k = 5). Motion wins only if ΔBIC > 10.

Parallax from orbit

Δα* = ϖ (X sin α − Y cos α)

X, Y, Z is the observer in au. Known asteroids are now looked up from the real position of SPHEREx in orbit (JPL Horizons id −163182), not from the ground.

Five questions for every change

  1. What changed?

    Three detectors read the same pixels: P, a catalogue of each visit; D, fair-pair differences; A, an anomaly ensemble of eight time features.

  2. Where?

    One grid anchored to Gaia stars, with measured position errors and SPHEREx's own spectral WCS for the colour of each pixel.

  3. Is it real?

    An artefact court: flags, crosstalk geometry 64 or 128 columns away, the twin array behind the dichroic, counterfactual reanalysis.

  4. What is it?

    Five hypotheses (moving, variable, transient, artefact, nothing) with calibrated probabilities and the right to say unknown.

  5. What would prove us wrong?

    The leading explanation is attacked with the tests most likely to break it before any word of the report is written.

Where each step lives in the code

On a practice sky with a hidden truth

The simulated sky follows the SPHEREx rules: six arrays, colours that step through each band, two arrays behind the dichroic at the same moment, and noise that the variance plane understates. It hides movers, variables, flares, glitches, hot pixels and crosstalk ghosts. The engine trained on some fields, three in ten of them deep six-visit fields like those near the ecliptic poles, and was scored on others it never saw.

Controls: 4 of 5599 catalogue stars with no motion were called moving; empty fields give 0.45 false candidates per field. These numbers hold for the practice sky only.

On the real SPHEREx sky

The real-data path runs on GitHub Actions against NASA IRSA. Native 2040 × 2040 DR1 exposures are downloaded with their spectral WCS, absolute gain and solid angle maps, checked against archive checksums, and used for calibrated injection and recovery. Each planted source carries its own photon noise.

Lupus III: an honest zero, explained

All 60 injections were missed, though the archive files, calibration links and checksums all pass. Every miss was a source outshone by a brighter neighbour: the cloud’s own sky is 5.5 times noisier than the pipeline variance says.

A zero turned into physics

The pipeline variance describes photons and electronics. It knows nothing about the cloud. In Lupus III, glowing dust is structured on the scale of a star image, so every planted source sat beside a brighter bump of real sky and lost to it.

Modelling that structured sky moves the honest 5σ limit in Lupus III up by a factor of 3.1. Planted at that limit, 12 of 40 are found instead of none. The primary rule stays as it was, and both answers are published.

A prediction made before looking

The sky-aware rule was built on the pilot fields, so those fields cannot test it. Before downloading six new fields (Chamaeleon I, Serpens, Corona Australis, NGC 2264, IC 348 and a southern control), the predictions and the rule for adopting it were committed to the repository: the preregistration.

The Planet X laboratory

A distant planet shows two motions. Seen from an observer circling the Sun at 1 au, it wobbles each year by a parallax of about 1/d radians, and it drifts slowly along its orbit. A published IRAS and AKARI pair sits 47.46 arcminutes apart after 23 years. If that drift is orbital motion on a circle, Kepler's third law puts it near 480 au.

μ ≈ 2π / P,   P = d3/2 years

Two positions do not make an orbit. The lab shows the ladder from prediction to candidate to detection to confirmed discovery, and states the measurement that would falsify each step. Nothing here is a discovery.

    What works, and what does not yet

    Works today

    • Search, download and read real SPHEREx QR3 and DR1 files, every extension, with checksums
    • Fair pairs, three detectors, artefact court, five-hypothesis engine with an unknown answer
    • Calibrated injection into real DR1 images with source photon noise, in seven fields
    • Known asteroids looked up from SPHEREx's own orbit; investigation cache with a size limit
    • Whole sky tiles with null and injection twins; Planet X lab; bilingual story
    • Python and MATLAB agree on the core physics to machine precision
    • A preregistered test on six new fields: all four predictions held in the five that completed, so noisy fields now report the sky-aware rule
    • A reproduction kit for an outside astronomer and a classroom kit with a worksheet and answer key

    Not yet

    • Real data is much harder: 24 percent of planted sources found in QR3, against 97 percent on the practice sky
    • Lupus III recovered 0 of 60 under the primary rule. The cause is structured nebular sky. The sky-aware rule now reported there gives an honest limit, not extra sensitivity, and NGC 2264 stays hard for both rules
    • No blind test held by an independent custodian. The reproduction kit is ready, but no outside astronomer has run it yet
    • Corona Australis stopped after 10 of 25 field checks in the held-out run; light curves for the 2,421 young stars are not built yet
    • The gain-to-electron conversion awaits review by an instrument scientist
    • QR3 covers about two months of most places, so slow movers barely move yet
    • Nothing here is a discovery. Every result is a candidate with its evidence attached

    Who it helps

    SPHEREx maps the whole sky in 102 colours every six months, and its archive is open to anyone. Students, amateur variable-star observers and researchers who want to check a claim about a changing source need a way to separate a real change from the instrument. CHRONOS gives them that test, the evidence behind it, and a plain-language report in English and Bangla.

    Done since the last release: the sky-aware noise rule passed a test declared in advance on new fields, the reproduction kit is written, and the classroom kit is on this page. Next: an outside astronomer runs the kit, the full DR1 release as SPHEREx completes its four sky surveys, and light curves for 2,421 young stars.
